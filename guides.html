Quick answer: Usually nothing. Coupon code, promo code and discount code all mean a code you enter at checkout to reduce an eligible price. The wording is a vendor habit, not a different product.

What each term usually means

A coupon code, a promo code and a discount code are all strings of letters and numbers entered in a box at checkout. If the code is valid and the items are eligible, the total goes down. Stores pick one word and stick with it, which is why you see all three in search results.

When the wording does matter

Three things are worth separating, because they behave differently:

  • Referral codes link your order to the person who referred you. They may or may not change the price, so check the total.
  • Automatic discounts need no code. The lower price is shown on the product page or in the cart.
  • Membership discounts come from an account tier, such as a free member tier, not from a code.

What to look at instead of the name

The label on the code matters less than its terms: the percentage, whether it is sitewide or limited to eligible products, whether it is for first orders only, and its status. Our coupon table shows each of these for every code.

FAQ

Is a promo code better than a coupon code?

No. They are two names for the same mechanism. Judge a code by its percentage and its terms.

Do I need a code if a product is already on sale?

Not for the sale price, which applies automatically. A code may or may not stack with it, so test it in your cart.

Quick answer: Check the offer type first. If the date is recent, the usual causes are an excluded product, a first-order-only code, another discount blocking it, or a typo.

1. The code has expired

Vendors end promotions without notice. Look at the status next to the code. A code still circulating online may have stopped working.

2. It is for new customers only

First-order codes are rejected on an account that has already ordered. Terms such as "welcome discount" or "new customer offer" usually mean this.

3. The product is excluded

"Eligible products" means selected items only. Sale items, bundles and kits are common exclusions. A sitewide code can still skip some categories.

4. Another discount is active

Some stores allow one offer per order. A sale price or a member discount can block a code. Try removing the other discount to see whether the code applies.

5. The vendor changed the code

Codes get renamed and retired. If a code stops working, look for a newer one for the same vendor on the coupon table.

6. The promotion ended

Seasonal and limited-time offers close on a set date. If the offer was tied to an event, it may simply be over.

7. The code was typed wrongly

Use the Copy button and paste the code. Remove any space at the start or end, and check for letters that look alike, such as O and 0.

A quick test before you pay

Add the item to your cart, enter the code and read the new total. If the total did not change, the code has not applied, whatever message appears.

Quick answer: Take the price after the coupon, divide by the milligrams in the vial, and add shipping before you divide if you want the true cost. The lowest price per mg wins the price comparison.

Why the biggest coupon can lose

A bigger percentage off a higher price can still cost more. In an example with made-up vendors, Vendor A takes 20% off $60 for a 10 mg vial: $48, or $4.80 per mg. Vendor B takes 10% off $45 for a 10 mg vial: $40.50, or $4.05 per mg. The smaller coupon wins.

The formula

Price after coupon = regular price x (1 - discount / 100).
Price per mg = price after coupon / vial size in mg.
With shipping = (price after coupon + shipping) / vial size in mg.

Seven things to line up

  1. The regular price before any code.
  2. The coupon and what it applies to.
  3. The vial size in mg, in the same unit for every vendor.
  4. The price per mg.
  5. Shipping and any free-shipping threshold.
  6. Whether a certificate of analysis exists for your lot.
  7. The product size and lot.

What price per mg cannot tell you

It compares cost only. It says nothing about purity or identity. For that, read the certificate of analysis for your specific lot. See our guide to reading a COA.

How to read a peptide COAMatch the lot number on the certificate to the lot on your vial, then look at who tested it, which methods were used, the…

Quick answer: Match the lot number on the certificate to the lot on your vial, then look at who tested it, which methods were used, the purity figure, an identity test and the lot.

Start with the lot number

A certificate belongs to a specific batch. If the lot number on the document does not match the lot on your vial, the document says nothing about what you received.

Who did the testing

A certificate can come from the vendor, a manufacturer or an independent lab. An independent lab with a named address, a report number and a signature is easier to verify than an unsigned in-house page. You can contact a lab to confirm a report it issued.

Typical sections

  • Purity: often reported as a percentage from HPLC. Look for the method and the chromatogram, not only the number.
  • Identity: often mass spectrometry, which checks that the measured mass matches the expected one.
  • Appearance, date and batch details.

What a COA does not prove

A COA does not show that a product is safe, legal for a given purpose or suitable for any use. It also covers only what was tested, on the sample that was tested. Treat it as one piece of evidence.

Red flags

  • No lot number, or a lot number that does not match.
  • No lab name, no date, or a document that looks edited.
  • A purity figure with no stated method.
  • The same certificate shown for several different products.

FAQ

Do vendors publish COAs?

Many list certificates on their product pages or in a COA library. Check the lot on the certificate against your vial.

Is a high purity number enough?

No. Check the lot, the method, the identity test and who ran it.

Sitewide vs eligible-product codesA sitewide code takes its percentage off the whole cart. An eligible-product code only reduces the items the vendor includes…

Quick answer: A sitewide code takes its percentage off the whole cart. An eligible-product code only reduces the items the vendor includes. In our table 26 of the 39 codes are sitewide and 13 are limited to eligible products, so it pays to know which one you are holding.

The difference in one line

With a sitewide code, 20% off a $100 cart is $20 off, whatever is in it (subject to any stated minimum). With an eligible-product code, 20% off applies only to the part of the cart the vendor allows. The percentage on the badge is the same, but the saving can be very different.

A worked example (invented numbers)

Take a $100 cart where $60 of it is on the eligible list and $40 is a bundle that the vendor excludes. A 20% eligible-product code saves 20% of $60, which is $12. A 10% sitewide code saves 10% of $100, which is $10. The smaller sitewide percentage is almost as good, and it would win if the eligible part of the cart were smaller.

How to tell which type you have

  • Look at the Offer column in our coupon table.
  • Read the vendor’s own banner or terms. Wording such as “selected products” or “excludes bundles” means eligible-product.
  • Test it: add one item, apply the code and watch whether the discount line appears.

Common exclusions

Sale prices, bundles, kits, blends and newly launched products are the items most often left out. A code can also have a minimum order or work only on a first order. Each one changes the real saving, so read the total, not the badge.

Which should you prefer?

Do not rank them by the percentage alone. Estimate the eligible part of your cart, multiply, then compare the result with the other vendor’s price per mg using the calculator.

FAQ

Can a sitewide code still skip some products?

Yes. Sitewide usually means the whole catalogue, but vendors can exclude sale items, bundles or gift cards. Confirm in the cart.

Do eligible-product codes work on sale prices?

Often not. Sale prices are a frequent exclusion, and many vendors allow only one discount per item.

Is a sitewide code always better?

No. A higher eligible-product percentage can save more if most of your cart is eligible. Do the sum for your own cart.

Quick answer: Research use only (RUO) means a product is sold for laboratory research and not for people or animals. It is not an approval, a safety statement or a legal shield, and regulators have acted against some sellers who market research peptides for personal use.

What the label means

An RUO label tells you the seller intends the product for laboratory research. These products are not approved medicines. They are not evaluated for safety or effectiveness in people, and they are not offered for human or veterinary use.

What it does not mean

  • It does not mean the product is safe, tested or pure. Only the paperwork for your lot can speak to purity and identity.
  • It does not make any use lawful. Rules differ by country, and the label does not override them.
  • It does not stop regulators from acting. Law-firm and policy commentary describes warning letters sent to some sellers of research peptides.

What a careful buyer can check

  • A lot-specific certificate of analysis from a named laboratory.
  • A vendor that keeps its marketing to laboratory research and does not make health claims.
  • Clear business details, shipping terms and a way to contact the seller.

Where this site stands

Peptide Discount lists prices and coupon codes. It does not sell products, test them or tell anyone to use a compound. Questions about health or about what is lawful where you live belong with a qualified professional or the relevant authority.

No. The label alone does not decide that, and regulators look at how products are marketed and sold.

Peptide vendor red flagsWarning signs include a COA with no lot number, no named testing lab, health or dosing claims on an RUO site, prices far below…

Quick answer: Warning signs include a COA with no lot number, no named testing lab, health or dosing claims on an RUO site, prices far below the rest of the market, and no clear contact or shipping terms. A coupon code is not a quality signal either way.

Paperwork red flags

  • No lot number on the certificate, or one that does not match your vial.
  • No named lab, or a lab you cannot find.
  • One certificate reused for different products.
  • A purity figure with no method or chromatogram.

Marketing red flags

  • Health claims. A research-only seller that describes benefits for people is a warning in itself.
  • Dosing instructions for research products.
  • Guaranteed or perfect purity claims such as “100% pure” with nothing behind them.

Business red flags

  • No contact details, no shipping policy and no returns terms.
  • Prices far below every other vendor for the same compound and size.
  • Pressure tactics such as countdown timers and constant new codes.
  • Only payment routes with no buyer protection.

Positive signs

Lot-specific reports from a named laboratory, clear shipping and handling information, a plain research-use-only statement and a vendor that answers questions about its paperwork before you buy.

What a coupon code tells you

Nothing about quality. A code lowers a price. Use it only after the vendor passes the checks above, then compare the final price per mg with the calculator.

FAQ

Is a cheap vendor a red flag?

Not automatically, but a price far below the market deserves a closer look at the paperwork.

Do many coupon codes mean a vendor is risky?

No. Many legitimate stores run codes. The paperwork matters far more.

Can you recommend a vendor?

No. This site lists codes and prices and does not rank vendor quality.

Quick answer: Compare four things next to the price: shipping cost and free-shipping threshold, the delivery window, where the vendor ships from, and how the package is handled. A cheaper vial can become the dearer order once shipping is added.

Shipping cost and thresholds

Shipping is usually billed separately from a coupon. Some vendors waive it above a minimum order, which can tip the comparison toward a vendor with a smaller discount. Add shipping to the price before you work out the cost per mg.

Delivery windows

Vendors quote windows such as 2 days or 3 to 7 days. These are estimates from the vendor and do not include processing time, so treat them as a guide. In the vendor data we hold, most quotes fall between 2 and 5 days, while a few say to contact the vendor.

Where it ships from

Almost every vendor in our list ships from the United States. A few ship from other countries, which can mean longer transit and possible customs checks. If the origin is not listed, ask before ordering.

Handling and storage on arrival

Research peptides usually arrive as freeze-dried powder. Check the vendor’s and the certificate’s storage guidance, inspect the package on arrival, and avoid leaving parcels in heat. This is general material handling and not advice on use.

Use the price per mg calculator with the shipping box filled in, so a vendor with free shipping and one without are compared fairly.

FAQ

Does a coupon code reduce shipping?

Usually not. Most codes apply to the item subtotal. Check the cart.

Is faster delivery worth paying for?

Only if it matters for your timeline. Compare the extra cost with the saving per vial.

What if the vendor does not list its delivery time?

Ask before you order. A missing delivery quote is worth treating as a question mark.
